The translational gap

Two problems stand between today’s models and the clinic. My work addresses them in three connected thrusts.

Benchmark vs. reality

Models excel on benchmarks but struggle with heterogeneous, real-world clinical data.

Operational limitations

Poor generalizability, limited interpretability, and a lack of clinical reasoning.

Overarching goal

Merge advanced neuroimaging and multi-modal AI tools for the in vivo, subject-specific investigation of brain-related disease.

A Next-generation neuroimaging AI tools

Clinically-aware lesion segmentation, and decoding the biological aging clock from structural MRI.

A1 Segment the lesion Clinically-aware lesion segmentation

Motivation

Clinically meaningful delineation requires task-specific clinical reasoning. In glioblastoma, extent of resection is a major determinant of survival, so millimeter accuracy matters. In acute ischemic stroke, rapid, reliable infarct-core estimation drives triage and treatment eligibility.

Approach
  • Re-DiffiNet (MIDL 2024, oral): diffusion models that explicitly model the discrepancies between U-Net predictions and the ground truth.
  • ISLES’24 pipeline: clinically-informed preprocessing (learning-based skull stripping and contrast-specific intensity windowing) with nnU-Net. It won 1st place (gold medal) in the ISLES’24 stroke challenge at MICCAI 2024; I was team leader and first author.
Key findings
  • Re-DiffiNet improved Dice by 0.55% and reduced Hausdorff distance by over 16%.
  • The ISLES’24 pipeline reached a mean Dice of 28.5 on the challenging ISLES’24 dataset.
  • Clinically-informed mechanisms outperform complex architecture changes.
Future directions

Agentic, clinically-reasoning models that decompose the problem into faithful, human-interpretable steps (Fig. 2):

  • a vessel-segmentation agent for vascular topology and occlusions;
  • a CT-perfusion agent using Fourier Neural Operators;
  • an anatomical-context agent using atlases and vascular territories;
  • all coordinated by a reasoning vision-language agent.
Equity

Low-resource settings: transfer learning for glioma segmentation in low-resolution data from Sub-Saharan Africa, and stroke lesion prediction from CT where MRI is unavailable.

A2 Quantify the aging Decoding the biological aging clock from structural MRI

I estimate global and regional brain age to characterize how an individual’s aging trajectory deviates from population norms.

Approach

An Age-Delta-Correlation (ADC) loss counters the “regression-to-the-mean” bias of brain-age models, finding the Pareto frontier between accuracy and systematic bias (ISBI 2026).

Preliminary findings

The brain-age gap in Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment associates with cognitive measures (MoCA, ADAS-Cog).

Future directions

Spatially resolved, subject-specific brain age from T1-weighted MRI, with Shapley attributions at the voxel and parcel level, to relate regionally accelerated aging to cognition and Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers.

B Trustworthy neuroimaging AI

Explanations that align model reasoning with clinical reasoning, and metrics that show when a model’s output can be trusted.

B1 Explaining segmentation Clinically grounded explanations for multi-contrast segmentation

  • Question 1

    Why and how does the model arrive at a prediction?

  • Question 2

    Under what conditions can clinicians trust its output?

Approach

I developed the first clinically grounded, contrast-level Shapley method for explaining multi-contrast medical image segmentation (XAI 2025). With radiologists, I extended it into a clinically interpretable evaluation framework (ISBI 2026) with two metrics:

  • Agreement: does the model’s contrast-importance ranking align with clinical imaging protocols?
  • Uncertainty: the variance of Shapley-derived rankings across folds.
Key findings
  • Transformer-based models give more consistent explanations than convolutional ones.
  • Correlation between model reasoning and clinical heuristics is a strong indicator of segmentation reliability.
  • Higher Shapley variance tracks performance degradation.
Future directions

Interactive, human-in-the-loop systems in which radiologists visualize contrast importance, calibrate uncertainty, and guide refinement.

Earlier work: computational biomechanics of fracture healing

During my Ph.D. at Lehigh University, advised by Prof. Hannah Dailey, I modeled how bone fractures heal:
